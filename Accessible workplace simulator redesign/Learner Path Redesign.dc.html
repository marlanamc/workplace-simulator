6a

Will this transfer to a real job?

Partly — and the honest answer matters more than a reassuring one, because it tells you what to build next. A simulator is necessary and insufficient. Below: what carries over, what doesn't, and the two additions that close most of the gap.

Transfers well
  • Permission to click. The belief that you can't break it is most of what's stopping your students, and a sandbox is the only place to build it.
  • The order of operations. Find who wrote to you → understand the ask → answer → attach. That sequence is identical everywhere.
  • Judgment, not pixels. View vs edit. Reply vs reply-all. Copy the template before you type. These are ideas; they survive any interface change.
  • Vocabulary in context. "Attach," "range," "view only," "cc" — learned while doing, not on a list.
  • Written workplace tone. Asking for a swap, calling out sick, writing up an incident.
Does not transfer
  • Getting in. Logins, wrong passwords, 2FA codes, "verify it's you." The most common real-world wall, absent here.
  • Where did the file go? Downloads, printing, a PDF opening in the wrong app, a photo taken on a phone that has to reach a computer.
  • Ambiguity. Every task here has one right file. Real work has four plausible ones and no highlight.
  • Unforgiving software. Real Gmail never says "that's Forward — try Reply." Coaching must eventually disappear or they'll expect a coach.
  • The device they own. If they practice on a simulated Chromebook and live on an Android phone, that's a second learning curve.

One more bias worth keeping

Transfer is strongest when the task is something the learner needs this month. Your Act I is excellent on that — reply to a manager, ask for a swap, read a pay stub, call out sick. Slide decks and expense reports in Acts VI–VII are aspiration, not need. Keep them, but never let them come before a student can request a day off in writing. If you only ever ship Act I, revised and with the bridge, your students are meaningfully more employable. That's the version I'd hand over.

5a

The story and the curriculum — honest read

The trajectory is genuinely good
  • Trunk, then doors. Everyone does New Hire → Shift Lead, then chooses stay / healthcare / office. That respects that your students want different lives. Most curricula force one ladder.
  • Tasks are moves, not app tours. "Share the file, not the folder" transfers to any employer. "Here is the Drive interface" does not.
  • One named manager the whole way. Maria is the spine. Keep her.
  • Job titles as chapter names. The reward is a role, not a level number.
Six things I'd change
  1. Level 1 is three skills in one. Find the right email + write a reply + use a file picker. For a low beginner that's a 12-minute cliff on day one. Split it into three 4-minute jobs.
  2. Level 2 bundles three tasks while its neighbours have one. Pacing lurches, and "about 10 minutes" stops being true.
  3. Nothing about life happening. "Tell Maria you can't come in today" is the highest-value message an hourly worker ever sends. It's currently only a handbook fact.
  4. Spreadsheets are overweight — 5 of ~20 trunk+stay tasks. Trade one for a web form with a dropdown and a date picker, which hourly workers hit constantly.
  5. No account recovery. Track 0 mentions login safety once. A learner who gets locked out in real life stops using computers. "You forgot your password. Get back in." deserves to be a real task.
  6. No review levels. 27 levels of always-a-new-thing. Adults consolidate through mixed practice — and a no-new-tools shift is exactly where the release ladder proves mastery.
What I'd do to Act I, concretely

Same content, more stops. Nine short jobs instead of six long ones — and every one of them ends inside a single sitting.

DAY ONE
1. Read Maria's email. Tell me what she wants. (no writing yet)
2. Write her back in your own words.
3. Send the same reply with the file in it.

Splitting reading from writing from attaching means a wrong answer tells you which of the three is missing.

THE FIRST WEEK
4. Find your shift on the schedule.
5. Two shifts overlap. Ask to swap. (new: web form)
6. You are sick. Tell Maria the right way. (new)

Job 6 is the one a student will use in real life within a month.

PAYDAY & TROUBLE
7. Clock out. Check the hours.
8. Read a pay stub. Is it right?
9. A normal shift. Three things you already know how to do. No new tools. (review)

Job 9 is what earns the promotion — you were tested, not just advanced.

Incident report and handbook move to Act II. Writing up an incident in professional English is a lead's task, not week one — it's the hardest writing job in your whole trunk. And the branch after Act II should arrive in the story: Maria offers you the lead job, or a job posting lands in your inbox. Not a menu of three doors.

Yes to a color per act — but on the room, not the apps

A green Gmail or an orange Drive would teach a student to recognize software that doesn't exist, and would read as "broken" to anyone who has seen the real thing. Keep every app pixel-neutral. Move the act color to the world around the apps: the wallpaper room, the session-start card, the My-job header, the Right-now bar accent, the certificate seal. Then read progression as time of day — morning light, mid-shift, evening, night at HQ. That's a promotion you can feel without a single label.

4a

Rewards for adults — proof, not points

The test I'd use: would a 45-year-old show this to their spouse, or hide it? Confetti on every email and a ★ score say "school game." A named skill, a note from your manager, and a certificate with your name on it say "I am becoming employable." Same dopamine, no condescension. Adults stay engaged for evidence of progress toward a real job — that is the fun.

Keep — these carry weight
  • Skill statements in first person — "I can answer my boss and send a file." Reads like a résumé line.
  • Maria's reply after each job. Human recognition from a named person beats any animation.
  • Promotions — New Hire → Shift Lead. Rare, earned, changes what you're allowed to do.
  • A printable certificate with their name, the date, and the skill list.
  • "No help used" marks on a skill — quiet proof of mastery.
Soften — keep, turn down
  • Points: keep the total, but inside My job — not a ★ counter in the taskbar with a "+100" pop.
  • Confetti: certificates and promotions only. Not every task, or it means nothing by task four.
  • Progress bar: label it in jobs and minutes ("Stop 2 of 17 · about 10 min"), not a percentage.
  • The awards case: real skill cards instead of an emoji trophy shelf.
Cut — these backfire
  • Streaks. An adult with two jobs and kids will break one, feel judged, and stop coming back.
  • Leaderboards / classmate comparison. Shame is not engagement, and literacy levels vary wildly.
  • Timers and speed scores. They punish exactly the careful reading you want to teach.
  • Lives, hearts, or losing progress. Wrong clicks must stay free.
  • Cartoon mascots. Maria is the character. She's a manager, and that's better.

The release ladder — four rungs, then they're on their own

Same task, four different amounts of help. The rung is a property of the skill, not the level: the first time a learner ever attaches a file they get rung 1, even at Level 9. Do it a second time and that skill drops to rung 2 on its own. So a Supervisor-level learner meeting a brand-new tool still gets pointed at it, and nobody re-reads instructions for something they've already done three times.

The net never comes off

At every rung, help comes back on its own if they need it — no button-hunting. Two wrong clicks, or 30 seconds with no click, and the bar slides back one rung for that step only: "Want me to show you?" Accept and the pointer returns. Nothing is ever locked behind knowing to ask.

Let them feel the release

Name it when it happens: "You did this one with no help." A rung-4 finish reads differently on the done screen — You did this like it is your job. Independence has to be visible, or they'll think the app just got worse.

And a way back down

Two failed runs of a skill, or a three-week gap between sessions, drops it back a rung quietly. Coming back after a month should feel like a warm start, not a test. Your Wednesday check-in view shows which skills slipped.

2a

Leading a low-literacy learner through it, alone

Don't know where to click

One card, one button. Nothing else is clickable on the home screen until they start.

What is it asking?

A permanent Right now bar: one sentence, 6–10 words, with the target's real icon. Never a paragraph.

When am I done?

Three visible ticks fill as they work. The bar turns green and says Done. No guessing.

Getting to the next task

One big green button, plus Stop for today — so a session has an ending, not a drop-off.

Too many shelf buttons

Six pins become three: Apps · My job · Me. Levels, awards and points move inside My job.

Guidance model: guided the first time, fading after. A task's first run has the Right-now bar plus a Show me highlight on the exact thing to click. On a second task of the same kind, the bar stays but the highlight only appears when they ask for it or sit still for 20 seconds. Every instruction has a speaker button and a Spanish twin.
